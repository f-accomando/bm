A short story.

The Lighthouse Keeper's Robot

At the end of a long gray beach stood a lighthouse that nobody visited anymore. The sea had moved away, the town had moved inland, and the only person who still climbed the stairs every evening was an old woman called Mara. She had kept the light for forty years, and she did not plan to stop now.

One rainy morning, while she was cleaning the lamp, Mara heard a quiet knock at the bottom of the tower. She walked down slowly, holding the railing, and opened the heavy door. On the step stood a small robot, no taller than a bucket, made of old metal and wrapped in a yellow raincoat that was clearly too big for it.

"Good morning," said the robot in a thin voice. "I am looking for a place where I can be useful. Do you have any work?"

Mara laughed, because nobody had asked her for work in years. "I have plenty of stairs," she said, "and the lamp needs polishing every day. But I cannot pay you."

"That is fine," said the robot. "I only need a dry corner and a little oil on Sundays."

So the robot stayed. At first it was clumsy, and it dropped the cloth more than once. It counted every step on the stairs aloud and found there were two hundred and twelve, which Mara had never known. It learned to polish the glass until it shone like a mirror, and it learned to make tea, although it could not drink any. In the evenings they sat together by the window, and Mara told stories about the ships she had seen, the storms she had survived, and the strange fish that once washed up on the sand.

One night in autumn a terrible storm arrived. The wind pushed against the walls, the rain hit the windows like thousands of tiny stones, and then, without any warning, the great lamp went dark. Mara tried the switch again and again, but nothing happened. Somewhere far away, in the black water, a small fishing boat was trying to find the coast.

"The wire in the lamp has burned out," said the robot. "I can feel it. Let me go up."

"It is too dangerous," Mara whispered. "The ladder is wet and old."

But the robot was already climbing. Step by step it reached the top, opened the lamp and looked inside. It had no spare wire, so it did the only thing it could think of. It took the small light from its own chest, the one that glowed softly whenever it was happy, and placed it carefully in the center of the great glass. The light was tiny, but the glass made it larger, and a clear beam went out over the dark waves.

Mara stood at the foot of the stairs and watched the beam turn slowly. Hours passed. The robot did not move, and its light grew weaker, but it never went out. Just before the morning came, a horn sounded in the distance, then another, and the little boat appeared out of the fog and slid safely into the harbor.

When the sun rose, Mara climbed to the top and found the robot sitting very still beside the lamp, its chest dark and its raincoat wet. She was afraid for a moment, but then she knelt, opened a small door on its back, and connected a new battery that she had kept in a drawer for years. Slowly, the little light returned.

"Did we save the boat?" asked the robot.

"We did," said Mara, smiling through her tears. "You saved it."

After that day the villagers began to come back to the beach. They brought bread, fresh paint and new wire for the lamp, and they liked to listen to the robot count the stairs. The lighthouse was never lonely again, and every evening, when the sky turned orange, two shadows climbed the tower together to light the lamp for anyone who might be lost out at sea.
